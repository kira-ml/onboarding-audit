# Problem Brief — Account-Opening Fraud Policy Evaluation

**Status:** v1.0.0, 2026-11-20.
**Purpose:** Define the problem this project solves, the user, the decision, the
evidence, and the limits. This is the document a fintech reviewer should read
first. The measured findings are in [`RESULTS.md`](RESULTS.md); the claim ledger
is [`CLAIMS.md`](CLAIMS.md).

---

## 1. The decision

A bank or e-wallet must decide, for each new account application, whether to:

- **approve** the applicant,
- **review** the applicant manually (document verification, KYC escalation,
  identity checks), or
- **reject** the applicant.

The onboarding policy that makes this decision is one of the highest-leverage
controls in the institution. It determines who enters the financial system, which
fraudulent applications are stopped, which legitimate applicants are excluded,
and how much manual review capacity is consumed.

The decision this project supports:

> **Should we approve, reject, or change the onboarding policy — and can we
> defend the expected-impact number to a risk committee, an internal auditor,
> and the regulator?**

## 2. Why the expected-impact number is hard to trust

The data needed to evaluate a new onboarding policy is generated by the current
policy.

### 2.1 Selection

- Applications the incumbent policy **reviews** get a fast, relatively reliable
  label (the review/KYC outcome).
- Applications the incumbent policy **approves** are labeled only if fraud is
  detected later — a chargeback, a customer report, a mule investigation. Most
  approved legitimate accounts are never labeled "legitimate."
- Applications the incumbent policy **rejects** have **no outcome at all**. The
  rejected population is structurally unobserved.

A naive evaluation on the labeled subset therefore measures the population the
incumbent policy chose to review or detect, not the population the new policy
would face.

### 2.2 Delay

- Review/KYC labels arrive in hours or days.
- Post-approval fraud labels arrive in weeks or months.
- Some labels are censored at the evaluation cutoff.
- A label that has not arrived is not a negative label; it is unresolved.

### 2.3 Capacity

Only a small fraction of applications can be manually reviewed. The review
capacity is a first-class constraint, and the review policy is part of the
decision being evaluated. A policy that looks better at unlimited capacity may
be worse at the capacity the institution actually has.

### 2.4 Audit

A random post-hoc verification of **approved** applications can recover unbiased
labels for the approved margin at the cost of review effort. A random approval
of would-be-rejected applicants would deliberately onboard fraud; a random
rejection of would-be-approved applicants would deliberately exclude legitimate
customers. Neither is a defensible audit design. The reject margin therefore
requires **bounds or explicit assumptions**, not a point estimate.

## 3. Why account opening, and why now (Philippines)

### 3.1 The data matches the decision

The public Bank Account Fraud (BAF) dataset is **one million online bank
account-opening applications**, generated from an anonymized real-world
account-opening fraud detection dataset (Jesus et al., NeurIPS 2022,
arXiv:2211.13358). It is not transaction authorization data. Evaluating an
account-opening policy on BAF is a direct task match. Pombal et al. (2022) used
the same setting to study selective labels and performative prediction
(arXiv:2206.13183), which confirms the setting's relevance and gives this
project a clear precedent to build on.

### 3.2 Fraudulent onboarding is the entry point for the dominant harms

- **Social engineering, account takeover, and identity theft = 76% of reported
  cyber-fraud losses in H1 2025** (BSP data reported by BusinessWorld/Philstar).
- **AMLC recorded 821,279 money-mule-related suspicious transaction reports
  worth PHP 510.17 billion** (Q1 2016–Q1 2022).
- **GCash blocked four million accounts in 2023**, over 900,000 in Q1 2023
  alone.
- **APAC synthetic identity data rose 142% year over year** (Sumsub, reported
  by Fintech News PH).
- **BSP consumer complaints: 70,112 in 2024 → >120,000 in 2025** (+72.6%);
  only **41.2%** of 2024 escalated complaints were resolved fully in the
  complainant's favour.

Fraudulent accounts enable mule networks and scams. A stricter onboarding
policy can reduce fraud but also reject legitimate, often lower-income
applicants — a financial-inclusion harm. That trade-off is exactly what a
cost-sensitive policy evaluation should measure.

### 3.3 The regulator requires demonstrable calibration and audit

- **BSP Circular No. 1170 (2023)** governs eKYC and digital identity
  proofing/authentication, including anti-fraud and cyber-security processes for
  AML/CFT.
- **BSP Circular No. 1213 (2025)** requires automated, real-time fraud
  monitoring; a Fraud Management System; rule-based **and machine-learning**
  approaches; **"constant calibration … through continuous data analysis, risk
  assessments, adaptive rule adjustments, machine learning refinements, regular
  stress testing, independent review and audits, and proactive monitoring of
  fraud patterns"**; and at least **5 years** of tamper-protected logs with
  **11 minimum fields**.
- **RA 12010 (AFASA), Section 6** makes institutions liable for **restitution**
  where risk management systems and controls are inadequate. **"Conviction
  shall not be a prerequisite to the restitution of funds."**
- **BSP Project Sapiens (2024)** found that fraud/AML is the **top AI use case**
  (25 of 48 respondents), names **Sampling Bias** and **Labelling Bias** in its
  bias taxonomy, reports that only **6 of 10** deep-dive institutions validate
  AI models during development and on an ongoing basis, and notes that some
  banks include only credit/lending — not fraud — in internal-audit and
  compliance reviews.
- **NPC Advisory 2024-04** and **NPC Circular 2022-04** impose binding
  explainability, human-intervention, contestability, bias-monitoring, and
  automated-decision registration duties under the Data Privacy Act.

The regulatory gap is concrete: **the 11 mandated log fields contain no
decision, score, propensity, or outcome field.** A team can satisfy the logging
rule and still be unable to evaluate its own onboarding policy.

### 3.4 The market gap

A capability review of 18 vendors found the following. It is a **search-based**
review of public documentation, not a proof of absence; the matrix is in the
archived origin repository and is not reproduced here.

- Replay backtesting is commoditized (IBM, Alloy, Sardine, Unit21; claimed by
  Feedzai/SAS).
- Champion–challenger is live traffic splitting, not causal estimation.
- Randomized audit arms are essentially absent as a product feature in the
  reviewed sources. Stripe is the only detailed public implementation of
  propensity-based random allows in those sources; Sardine discusses 1% control
  groups as an advanced practice requiring scale and loss budget.
- No vendor publicly documents, **in the reviewed sources**, propensity logging
  as a first-class field, label-delay distributions, overlap/ESS diagnostics,
  audit-power/loss-budget tooling, capacity-constrained review optimization, or
  an audit memo that separates measured/assumed/unverifiable claims.
- No fraud-specific OPE library was found in the 2026 search. General OPE (Open
  Bandit Pipeline, SCOPE-RL), causal (EconML, DoWhy), credit reject inference
  (`rejectkit`), and debt-collection OPE (JUBILEE) exist, but none combines
  account-opening actions, delayed labels, selection, and review capacity.
- `rejectkit`'s own benchmark warns that selection corrections can **hurt**
  under MNAR (naive AUC 0.749 vs oracle 0.820). A credible tool needs negative
  controls and honesty checks, not just an estimator.

### 3.5 The research gap

The targeted review of 25 primary sources found:

- **Selective labels** are well established (Lakkaraju et al. 2017; Kleinberg
  et al. 2018; Ensign et al. 2018; Perdomo et al. 2020), but the established
  remedies identify relative performance or rely on quasi-random assignment;
  they do not solve a new policy with no overlap.
- **Reject inference** in credit scoring has a weak empirical track record
  (Crook & Banasik 2004; Ehrhardt et al. 2021); recent work shifts value to
  evaluation-side correction and controlled exploration (Kozodoi et al. 2025;
  Scarone & Baeza-Yates 2026; 2–5% exploration can diagnose the loop). This is
  the closest analogue to account-opening selection.
- **PU learning** is identifiable under SCAR, but fraud violates SCAR;
  calibration is fragile while ranking can survive (Bourazas et al. 2026).
- **Delayed feedback** is modelable, but fraud feedback is delayed, censored,
  corrupted, and counterfactually missing (Chapelle 2014; Dal Pozzolo et al.
  2015; Vernade et al. 2017; Dhama 2026).
- **OPE** is mature but its core assumptions — positivity, unconfoundedness —
  are exactly the ones fraud violates; Namkoong et al. (2020) show small
  per-decision confounding can heavily bias OPE, using human fraud screening as
  the motivating example. JUBILEE (2026) shows the same failure mode in debt
  collections: hidden assignment dependence makes log-based intervals miss.
- **Fraud rule portfolios** have a separate 2026 certification literature
  (selection-aware certification under synthetic distribution shift), which
  addresses overfitting in rule selection but not the account-opening reject
  margin.
- **The closest peer-reviewed 2026 work** — OSCAR (ICAIF 2026), Beyond Holdout
  Error (ICAIF 2026), and DOiT (ICAIF 2026) — studies selective labels,
  estimator-ranking reversal, and account-selection trade-offs in financial
  screening. None provides the full account-opening combination: a `P0`
  estimand, reject-margin bounds, a capacity layer, an audit design, an
  identification gate, and an open reproducible tool. Pombal et al. (2022)
  study bias and fairness in performative prediction, but do not provide a
  policy-evaluation toolkit, delay-aware comparison, capacity layer, audit
  design, or benchmark.

## 4. Research question and hypotheses

**Primary question:**

> When the onboarding policy chooses which applications are reviewed, approved,
> or rejected, how biased is the naive policy-value estimate — and which
> estimator, bound, or audit design makes the estimate defensible?

**Pre-registered hypotheses (see `PILOT_SPEC.md`):**

- **H1 (bias):** Naive evaluation on the labeled subset has a relative bias
  greater than 5% for at least one candidate policy under the main scenario.
- **H2 (ranking):** The naive estimate reverses at least one pairwise ranking
  of candidate policies relative to the true ranking.
- **H3 (correction):** At least one of IPW or DR has a **maximum absolute
  relative bias across the four policies <= 5%** and **mean 95% CI coverage
  >= 80%** on the identified population. Audit-only is reported separately
  against the approved population.
- **H4 (audit value):** Increasing the random post-hoc verification audit from
  0% to 2% reduces the RMSE of the corrected estimate and increases effective
  sample size, with diminishing returns.
- **H5 (reject margin):** The full-population value of a policy that changes
  the rejection rate is not point-identified from logs alone; the bounds are
  wider than the decision-relevant effect unless an assumption or a controlled
  approval design is added.
- **H6 (outcome-dependent labels):** When approved fraud is more likely to be
  labeled than approved legitimate accounts, action-only IPW is biased;
  audit-only is unbiased for the approved population, and the gap is detectable
  from the label-source mix.

## 6. What would change the conclusion

- The naive bias is negligible and no ranking flips under all realistic
  scenarios.
- The corrected estimators only work because the propensities are oracle-known;
  estimated or covariate propensities remove the advantage.
- The audit design is infeasible or legally unavailable.
- The reject-margin bounds are so wide that no decision can be supported.
- The binding operational problem is scam intervention UX, not onboarding
  policy evaluation.
- A vendor ships the same measurement layer with documented mechanics.

## 7. Scope and non-goals

**In scope (v0.2):**

- Account-opening approve/review/reject decisions on BAF.
- Action-dependent observation, delayed labels, censoring.
- Naive, IPW, DR, audit-only, bounds.
- Capacity-constrained review.
- Audit design (post-hoc verification, shadow scoring).
- Arbitrary-log report, action-level and covariate propensity models.
- Propensity and covariate sensitivity studies.
- A decision memo and claim ledger.

**Out of scope (v0.2):**

- A new fraud detector or classifier.
- A production integration or vendor replacement.
- A causal deployment claim.
- A random approval/rejection experiment.
- A Philippine proprietary dataset.
- A full FiFAR analyst simulation (planned as a follow-on).
- Scam/APP intervention evaluation (planned as a future extension if a bank
  partner provides data).
